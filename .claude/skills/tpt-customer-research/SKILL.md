# tpt-customer-research Skill — TPT Customer Research

Methodology for interviewing dads new to family court — recruitment, conducting interviews, synthesis, and translating findings into product/course/copy decisions.

## Outcome
- Recruitment plan (where dads come from + permission/consent flow)
- Interview guide tailored to where each dad sits on the trail (pre-filing / served / mid-divorce / post-decree)
- Recording + transcription pipeline that respects emotional safety and anonymization
- Synthesis doc with themes, surprises, direct quotes (anonymized), and product/copy implications
- A "what we believed vs. what they told us" delta that drives course outline decisions

## Why this matters

TPT is being built for dads in active emotional crisis. Bad research methodology produces two failures: (a) missing what they actually need because we asked leading questions, (b) re-traumatizing the participant by handling sensitive topics carelessly. This skill is the gate between assumption-driven product and dad-grounded product.

Phase 0 milestone: 8–12 conversations by 2026-05-16. Course outline (due 2026-05-23) is downstream and should not be drafted until at least 6 of these conversations are synthesized.

## Step 1 — Recruit

**Channels (in priority order):**
1. **Personal network referrals.** Ask Victor + buddy + their networks for "any dads you know who've been through family court in the last 24 months and would talk to me for 30 minutes." Warm intros convert at >50%; cold outreach <10%.
2. **Men's groups, faith communities, sports parent networks.** Posting in groups where dads gather, with a clear ask: "I'm researching what dads new to family court wished they'd known. Would value 30 minutes with anyone who's been through it. Not selling anything."
3. **Reddit communities** (with mod permission): r/divorce, r/Custody, r/Mensrights (CAREFUL — has a wide range of voices, screen for the dad-on-the-trail type), r/Dads.
4. **Existing TPT waitlist** (when one exists). Highest-quality but not available yet.

**Screening criteria (minimum):**
- Has been through family court within the last 24 months OR is currently within 6 months of filing/being served
- Is willing to talk on a recorded call for 30 min
- Is in a current state (not actively suicidal, not so deep in active litigation that talking is harmful)

**What we're NOT looking for:**
- "Dad's rights activists" with rigid pre-formed narratives
- Lawyers or advocates pretending to be dads
- Dads who are still mid-acute crisis (give them resources and follow up later)

## Step 2 — Consent and emotional-safety setup

**Before each interview, send a written one-pager:**
- What this is: research for a coaching/education service for dads new to family court
- What we'll talk about: their experience navigating the process, what they wish they'd known
- Recording: yes, audio only, transcribed for our internal synthesis only, never published verbatim, never shared outside the team
- Anonymization: name redacted, county redacted, identifying details redacted in any quote we ever use externally
- Their rights: stop the interview at any time, skip any question, withdraw consent after the interview within 7 days
- Compensation: a $50 gift card or coffee on us
- Not legal advice: we are not lawyers, we are not advising on their case

**Get explicit verbal consent on the recording before substantive questions begin.** "Are you okay with this being recorded for our internal synthesis only, with the protections we discussed?"

## Step 3 — Interview methodology

**Borrow heavily from Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*:**
- Ask about their life, not your idea. Don't pitch TPT. Don't describe the framework. Don't ask "would you pay for X" — ask "what did you actually pay for, and was it worth it?"
- Ask about specifics, not generalities. Not "what was hard?" but "walk me through the morning of [the day you got served]."
- Ask about past behavior, not hypothetical future behavior. "Future-tense answers are useless." (Fitzpatrick)

**Layer trauma-informed practice:**
- Start with the easiest part of their story (when they first knew something was happening) before asking about the hardest part (custody hearings, evaluations).
- Use silence. After a hard answer, do not jump in. Let them keep going if they want.
- Validate without amplifying. "That sounds hard. Take a breath." NOT "That's awful, you must be devastated."
- Watch for spiral signs (rapid speech, voice cracking, repetitive returning to one event). If you see them, slow down, return to a concrete logistical question, or offer a break.

**Don't:**
- Don't lead. Not "did you find lawyer fees expensive?" Yes "what did the lawyer fees look like, week to week?"
- Don't ask about mom. The interview is about HIS experience. Even if he brings her up, redirect: "And in that moment, what were you doing?"
- Don't fix. Resist the urge to share what you know or to coach during the interview. Take notes; the temptation to teach is the temptation to confirm your assumptions.
- Don't promise. "We're going to fix this for guys like you" is both a leading prompt AND a UPL-adjacent outcome promise. Avoid.

## Step 4 — Interview guide (tailor by stage)

**Opening (5 min) — same for everyone:**
- Thank you, why I'm here, consent confirmation, recording on
- "Tell me a little about yourself — work, kids, where you live in broad strokes."

**Their story (15 min) — let them lead:**
- "When did you first realize the family-court process was going to be part of your life?" Listen.
- "Walk me through the first 30 days after that."
- "What did you do, and in what order? What did you wish you'd done differently?"

**Money and time (5 min):**
- "What did you spend money on related to the case? Walk me through it."
- "Of what you spent, what felt worth it? What didn't?"
- "How much time did this take from your week, in the heaviest months?"

**Information-seeking (5 min):**
- "Where did you go for information? What did you wish existed that didn't?"
- "Did you read or buy anything that helped? That didn't help?"
- "Who in your life was most useful to talk to?"

**Closing (3 min):**
- "If a friend got served tomorrow, what's the one thing you'd tell him?"
- "Anything I didn't ask that I should have?"
- Thank you. Confirm 7-day withdrawal window. Send gift card.

**Stage variants:**
- Pre-filing dads: emphasize what they were tracking before, what they thought was coming
- Served dads: heavy on the first-30-days story, the documentation question
- Post-decree dads: heavy on what they wish they'd done in pre-decree phase, what they're doing now

## Step 5 — Recording and anonymization

- Record on Otter.ai or similar (cheap, auto-transcribes)
- Save audio + transcript to a folder named by interview number, not the dad's name (e.g., `interviews/2026-05-09-D03/`)
- Maintain a SEPARATE encrypted document mapping interview numbers to real names. Never share that document outside the team.
- In any synthesis or copy that ever leaves the team, redact: name, exact dates, exact county/court, employer, opposing party's name, attorney's name, any detail that could uniquely identify the case.
- Quote use rule: even with anonymization, ask permission before using any specific quote in marketing. Don't rely on the consent form alone for that — circle back.

## Step 6 — Synthesis

After each block of 4–6 interviews, run a synthesis pass:

**Code the transcripts:**
- Highlight quotes that surprise you (this is gold)
- Tag everything by topic: capture, organize, reflect, execute, money, time, info, emotional, advice
- Note recurring phrases — exact words multiple dads used (these become marketing copy and FAQ language)

**Produce a synthesis doc with these sections:**
1. **What dads consistently said** (>50% mentioned) — the validated assumptions
2. **What surprised me** — the things we didn't see coming. These are the gold.
3. **What dads disagreed about** — the spectrum of experiences (single dads vs. dads with custody, high-conflict vs. amicable, etc.). The course needs to account for this range.
4. **The exact phrases dads used** for the things we previously called "Capture / Organize / Reflect / Execute" — does our framework name the same things they name? If not, do we change the framework name or stick with ours and translate?
5. **What they wished they'd known** — sequenced by phase. This is the course outline raw material.
6. **What they paid for that didn't help** — the negative space; tells us what NOT to build.
7. **Direct anonymized quotes** (with their permission) — these power the website testimonials section, FAQ language, and ad copy.

**Cross-check against assumptions:**
- Compare the synthesis to the brand-promise assumptions in `context/SOUL.md` and the four pillars in `project_tpt_value_pillars`. Where does dad reality match? Where does it diverge?
- If divergence is significant (>30% of dads named a major need we didn't have a pillar for), bring to Victor — possible 5th pillar or pillar rename.

## Step 7 — Translate to product

The synthesis feeds directly into:
- **Course outline (Phase 0 milestone, 2026-05-23):** module sequence and content priorities follow from "what dads wished they'd known."
- **SOUL.md voice refinement:** if dads consistently use specific phrases ("just trying to do right by my kids," "didn't want to feel like a deadbeat"), update the voice guide to integrate those.
- **Lead magnet content** ("First 7 Things to Do"): the 7 things should be the most-mentioned wished-they'd-known items.
- **FAQ copy:** the exact questions dads asked us in interviews become the FAQ questions on the site (per tpt-aeo-seo: "questions a real user would ask").
- **Pricing positioning:** what dads said they'd pay for that didn't help, vs. what they wished existed, calibrates the founding-member pricing.

## Anti-patterns

- Pitching TPT during the interview. (You're researching, not selling.)
- Asking "would you pay for this?" — future-tense, leading. Ask about past spend instead.
- Talking more than 25% of the time. The dad should talk 75%+.
- Combining interview and coaching. Don't slip into advice mode mid-interview.
- Skipping the consent step because the dad seems fine with it verbally. Get it written.
- Synthesizing alone. Get a second pass from Victor or buddy on the themes — pattern-matching is more reliable with two readers.
- Letting one loud outlier dominate the synthesis. Track who said what and how often.
- Cherry-picking quotes that confirm what we already believed. The point of research is to be wrong sometimes.
